# The Lunch Desk — First Customers Playbook

The website and Google profile are infrastructure. They don't produce customers
on their own, especially in month one when nobody is searching for you yet.
Your first 5–10 customers will come from **direct outreach**. This is the kit.

> ⚠️ Everything here follows the same rules as the site: never promise delivery,
> never claim partnerships or clients you don't have, never quote a price you
> can't honour. You have no track record yet — your pitch is *saved time*, not
> social proof. Don't invent social proof.

---

## Who to approach first (highest → lowest odds)

**1. Pharmaceutical & medical sales reps.** The single best target. Buying lunch
for offices is literally part of their job, they do it weekly, they hate the
logistics, and it's expensed — so price sensitivity is low. One rep can become
recurring volume, and reps talk to each other.
*Find them:* they're standing in medical office waiting rooms late morning.

**2. Medical & dental offices.** Order lunch often (rep-sponsored and their own),
have an office manager who owns the task, and are clustered together so you can
walk several in one trip.

**3. Law firms, insurance, real estate, property management.** Client meetings and
staff lunches. An office manager or paralegal usually gets stuck with it.

**4. Car dealerships.** Frequent team lunches, sales meetings, big headcounts.

**5. Construction / trades offices.** Big crews, tight schedules, low fuss.

**Ask for the office manager.** Not the owner, not reception — the office manager
is who actually feels this pain and often controls the small budget.

---

## The walk-in (your strongest channel)

Best time: **9:00–10:30am** — before the lunch scramble, after they've settled.
Never 11:30–1:30, you'll be interrupting exactly what you're selling against.

Bring flyers (`brand/flyer-letter.png`).

> "Hi — I'm Kyle, I run The Lunch Desk here in [Myrtle Beach / Conway].
> We're a local service that handles office lunch ordering for businesses —
> you tell us the date, headcount and budget, we bring back options from local
> restaurants, place the order and coordinate pickup.
>
> Is your office manager around? I'll leave this either way."

If you get the office manager:

> "Quick question — when your office orders lunch for a group, who ends up
> organizing it?"

*(Let them answer. They will almost always name a person and sigh.)*

> "That's exactly what we take off your plate. No app, no account, no contract
> — you just text or call me the details and I handle the restaurant side.
> Next time you've got a team lunch or a meeting to feed, try me once and see
> if it's easier."

**Then stop talking.** Leave the flyer. Don't oversell — the pitch is that
trying you costs them nothing.

## Restaurant partner outreach

You need supply as well as demand. Go **mid-afternoon (2–4pm)** — never during
a rush. Ask for the owner or manager.

> "Hi — I run The Lunch Desk, we coordinate office lunches for businesses around
> here. I'm not selling anything: I'm looking for kitchens that can handle
> group orders for offices — boxed lunches, trays, that kind of thing.
>
> When I've got an office looking for [their cuisine], I'd like to be able to
> send it your way. Who's best to talk to about large orders, and do you have
> a group-order minimum I should know about?"

Then capture: contact name, direct number, group capacity, minimums, lead time
needed, and whether they do trays or individual boxes. **Put real partners into
`src/data/restaurants.ts`** (set `placeholder: false`) — that's what turns the
Lunch Options page from placeholders into a real directory.

## Handling the three objections you'll actually hear

**"What does it cost?"**
> "Depends on the order — I'll quote you before anything is placed, and you
> approve it first. Asking costs nothing."

Do NOT invent a fee structure on the spot. Decide your model, then be consistent.

**"We already have a place we use."**
> "That's fine — keep them. I'm useful when you need something different, or
> when you don't have time to organize it. I can work with the place you already
> like too."

**"Do you deliver?"**
> "I coordinate the order and the pickup. Some restaurants deliver and I'll
> arrange that when they do — but I won't promise delivery I can't control."

Be exact here. Over-promising delivery is the fastest way to a bad first review.

## After the first order — the most important 60 seconds

New local businesses live or die on Google reviews. The moment an order goes
well, while they're happy:

> "Glad that worked out. If you've got a second, a quick Google review genuinely
> helps a new local business — I'll text you the link."

Then actually text it. **Get your review link** once verified: Google Business
Profile → *Ask for reviews* → copy link.

Never pay for reviews, never review yourself, never offer discounts for reviews
— all of it violates Google's policies and can get the profile suspended.

---

## A realistic first-month target

- 30 offices walked in, flyer left
- 10 office managers actually spoken to
- 5 reps contacted
- 8 restaurants asked about group orders
- **2–3 first orders**

Two happy customers with two Google reviews is a real business. That's the goal
— not a hundred leads.

---

## Track it

You don't need a CRM. A spreadsheet with: business name, contact, role, date
contacted, channel, response, follow-up date, outcome. Follow up **once** after
a week — one follow-up roughly doubles response rates and doesn't annoy anyone.
